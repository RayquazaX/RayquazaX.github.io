1Procedure-Aware Reinforcement Learning for Tool-Augmented Large Language Models

Training tool-augmented large language models has evolved from supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). Yet existing methods rarely examine whether intermediate execution logic—specifically, the procedural fidelity of multi-turn tool invocations—requires explicit supervision. We hypothesize that enforcing sequential validity at the turn level is a prerequisite for robust multi-turn task completion. To this end, we introduce a Procedure-Aware Reinforcement Learning Framework that explicitly evaluates procedural validity through both invocation chain quality and final system state correctness. Using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with KL regularization to fine-tune Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, we demonstrate on API-Bank, Bamboogle, and BFCL-V3 that our approach consistently outperforms correctness-only baselines. Despite operating at a substantially smaller scale, our 3B model achieves average accuracy competitive with proprietary models such as GPT-5-mini, GPT-4.1-mini, Claude-Haiku-4.5, and Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite, whose scores are obtained under BFCL’s official per-model evaluation protocols. Qualitative analysis reveals that optimizing tool sequencing yields emergent benefits, simultaneously enhancing multi-turn performance and error recovery from failed tool calls.

Qinglong Zheng*, Jiajun Fan*, Chaoran Cheng, Ge Liu (* equal contribution)

A procedure-aware reward built on NDCG and suffix-based sequence matching. It rewards valid tool-invocation order while leaving room for exploratory prefixes, on top of KL-regularized GRPO.

65.67% on BFCL-V3 with a 3B model (ToolRL: 63.07%) · multi-turn 3.88% → 7.25%

2Decision Models Are General-Purpose Process Reward Models

Reinforcement learning (RL) from verifiable outcomes gives a language agent one reward per episode: every step is credited alike, and nothing is learned when all rollouts fail. Step-level credit usually needs step labels, a learned critic, costly LLM judges or hand-written rules. We introduce DM-PRM, which turns a general decision model – one answering yes/no questions with probabilities – into a general process reward model (PRM), zero-shot: no step labels, no fine-tuning, no critic. DM-PRM asks one question per step and adds the standardised answers to the advantage. It is plug-and-play for any policy-gradient method and general: one model serves household tasks, web shopping and multiple RL methods. With Qwen2.5-1.5B it lifts GRPO by over twenty points on ALFWorld and over ten on WebShop, overtakes GiGPO on ALFWorld and learns in far fewer iterations; it also improves GiGPO and GraphGPO on both benchmarks and reaches the highest success of our comparison, ahead of critic-based PPO and of state-of-the-art GiGPO, HGPO and GraphGPO. Its scores locate the useful steps of failed rollouts, and trained agents drop idle actions and explore instead of circling. DM-PRM shows that step credit need not be learned: it can be asked for.

Jiajun Fan, Qinglong Zheng, Ziwen Wang, Bangji Yang, Neil He, Ruihan Guo, Chaoran Cheng, Ge Liu

Turns a fixed general decision model into a zero-shot process reward model: it scores each action's progress from the complete execution log and its outcome, and the standardized scores are added to GRPO's outcome-based advantage. No step labels, no scorer training, no value critic.

Lifts GRPO by over 20 points on ALFWorld and over 10 on WebShop with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct

3STaR-VL: Capability-Aligned Reinforcement Learning for Visual-Temporal Memory

Vision-language models can recognize objects in individual images yet struggle to retain and compare observations across a changing scene. We present STaR-VL, a staged post-training framework for visual-temporal memory on a frozen Qwen2.5-VL backbone. Three LoRA adapters learn per-frame perception, symbolic temporal memory through a recurrent memory module, and transfer to visual histories, respectively; each stage combines supervised fine-tuning with a capability-aligned reinforcement-learning reward. Our contributions are a capability-aligned training framework and a controlled evaluation separating its gains from in-domain joint training, persistent-memory augmentation, and inference-time prompting. On SpaMEM, STaR-VL-3B reaches a V-score of 47.60, compared with 21.85 for the untrained backbone, 40.80 for a joint rank-48 LoRA, and 38.90 for an adapted ReMEmbR system on the same backbone. A structured memory-table prompt raises the untrained model to 22.21. The same trained checkpoints transfer to held-out benchmarks: the 7B model improves VSI-Bench from 33.3 to 46.4, with smaller gains on EgoSchema and TempCompass. Visual-history and symbolic-history controls reveal complementary effects of frame coverage and accessible scene state. These results support staged capability-aligned training for object recall, event detection, and cumulative memory over explicitly ordered observations, while identifying fine-grained grounding and temporal localization as remaining challenges.

Chih-Ting Liao, Zhikai Pan, Qinglong Zheng, Xin Cao

A staged post-training framework that pairs supervised fine-tuning with capability-aligned reinforcement learning, teaching a vision-language model perceptual grounding and visual-temporal memory.

47.60 SpaMEM V-score with Qwen2.5-VL-3B · 7B VSI-Bench 33.3 → 46.4 without additional training

4When to Ask, When to Act: Persona-Conditioned Clarification for Multi-Turn Human-LLM Collaboration

An LLM that asks too many clarifying questions interrupts the user; one that asks too few makes incorrect assumptions. Each clarification therefore requires deciding whether the value of resolving uncertainty outweighs the cost of interrupting the user, and the right answer depends on who is being asked. Yet existing methods optimize when to clarify based on task signals alone, leaving this user-dependence implicit. We introduce TactfulLLM, a lightweight training framework that makes this user-dependent question of when to ask and when to act an explicit learning target. We formalize this Clarify-or-Execute decision as cost-sensitive information acquisition under partial observability, with the policy conditioning on both task uncertainty and user persona. We optimize this policy via preference learning over simulator-generated multi-turn trajectories, where a turn-level score combining realized task success with the immediate persona-dependent interruption cost serves as a tractable surrogate for a cumulative-cost trajectory objective. On conversational code generation, TactfulLLM recovers nearly half (48%) of the performance gap caused by incomplete task specifications, adapting its behavior across personas: extended dialogue for patient users and direct execution for time-constrained ones. Our findings highlight complementary empirical roles for user persona and task uncertainty in learned clarification policies: persona information primarily shapes interaction length, while uncertainty information improves downstream task success. This provides a controlled analysis of persona-adaptive interaction behavior under simulated user-response dynamics.

Shanghao Li, Haojian Li, Jinda Han, Yibo Wang, Henry Peng Zou, Liuchuan Yu, Yuanjie Zhu, Zihe Song, Yaozu Wu, Wei-Chieh Huang, Weizhi Zhang, Langzhou He, Jie Yang, Qinglong Zheng, Mengyao Guo, Philip S. Yu

A training framework that makes the choice between asking a clarifying question and acting an explicit learning target. The policy conditions on task uncertainty and on who the user is, and is trained by preference learning over simulated multi-turn dialogues.

Closes 48% of the pass@1 gap caused by incomplete task specifications in conversational code generation

5SKT: Self-Evolving Agent Skill Library with Plan-Driven Test-Time Self-Correction

Long-horizon LLM agents must plan and act over many environment turns, yet adapting them by updating parameters is costly and often impossible behind an API. Inference-time learning from the agent’s own trials is the standard alternative, but memory-based methods mostly store experience as reflections, insights or context-resident rules, code-based skill libraries apply only where actions are code, and step-level verifiers deliver their verdicts through the prompt, a channel that a small frozen policy can attenuate or ignore. To address this, we propose SKT, which evolves a natural-language procedural skill library from the agent’s own successes and failures, consolidates failures across trials into new skills, and seats the same frozen weights in a separate verifier context whose corrections are executed directly as environment actions. The two engines are separable, and we evaluate each against its own controls rather than claiming that either depends on the other. Under a matched evaluation protocol with a frozen Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, SKT consistently outperforms inference-time baselines on held-out ALFWorld tasks, approaches an RL-fine-tuned reference without any weight update, and ports unchanged to multi-hop QA and, on a commercial API backbone, to WebShop.

Qinglong Zheng, Jiajun Fan, Jiashuo Sun, Bangji Yang, Ge Liu

A training-free framework in which a frozen agent creates, refines and distills natural-language skills from its own trials, while a separate verifier issues plan-driven corrections that are executed as environment actions.

58.96 ± 1.50% on 134 held-out ALFWorld tasks, training-free on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct

6Where Do Loops Help? Reallocating Test-Time Compute in Looped Diffusion Transformers

Looped diffusion transformers apply shared Transformer blocks repeatedly within each denoising step, so the loop depth can be chosen at inference without changing the parameter count. Current inference uses the same loop depth at every step, adaptive looping needs a trained gating network, and where additional loops actually help remains unclear. We study this question on Looped-DiT and find a simple pattern. Loops improve performance only up to the training loop depth, and only in the early denoising steps, while the layout of the image is being determined. This finding gives two training-free changes to inference. A front-loaded loop schedule applies the full depth early and a single loop afterwards. It cuts inference compute by more than a quarter without degrading performance on T2I-CoReBench, GenEval and DPG-Bench, and matches adaptive looping on T2I-CoReBench. We propose LoopScout, which reallocates the saved compute to additional candidates, scores their intermediate clean-image predictions at the switch step with a zero-shot checklist verifier, and completes only the best one. Under matched inference compute it consistently outperforms best-of-N sampling, and on the public B/16 checkpoint it matches best-of-4 with less than half of its inference compute.

Qinglong Zheng, Jiajun Fan, Chih-Ting Liao, Chaoran Cheng, Ruihan Guo, Bangji Yang, Shanghao Li, Jinda Han, Ge Liu

Extra loops in a frozen Looped-DiT help only in early denoising and only up to the training loop depth. A front-loaded schedule saves the wasted compute, and LoopScout spends it on more candidates that are verified and pruned from their intermediate previews.

28% less generator compute at no quality loss · 91.6 GenEval vs 90.6 for best-of-4 at 48% of its compute

7Retrieval is Cheap, Show Me the Code: Executable Multi-Hop Reasoning for Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has become a standard approach for knowledge-intensive question answering, but existing systems remain brittle on multi-hop questions, where solving the task requires chaining multiple retrieval and reasoning steps. Key challenges are that current methods represent reasoning through free-form natural language, where intermediate states are implicit, retrieval queries can drift from intended entities, and errors are detected by the same model that produces them making self-reflection an unreliable, ungrounded signal. We observe that multi-hop question answering is a typical form of step-by-step computation, and that this structured process aligns closely with how code-specialized language models are trained to operate. Motivated by this, we introduce PyRAG, a framework that reformulates multi-hop RAG as program synthesis and execution. Instead of free-form reasoning trajectories, PyRAG represents the reasoning process as an executable Python program over retrieval and QA tools, exposing intermediate states as variables, producing deterministic feedback through execution, and yielding an inspectable trace of the entire reasoning process. This formulation further enables compiler-grounded self-repair and execution-driven adaptive retrieval without any additional training. Experiments on five QA benchmarks (PopQA, HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, MuSiQue, and Bamboogle) show that PyRAG consistently outperforms strong baselines under both training-free and RL-trained settings, with especially large gains on compositional multi-hop datasets.

Jiashuo Sun*, Jimeng Shi*, Yixuan Xie, Saizhuo Wang, Jash Rajesh Parekh, Pengcheng Jiang, Zhiyi Shi, Jiajun Fan, Qinglong Zheng, Peiran Li, Shaowen Wang, Ge Liu, Jiawei Han (* equal contribution)

An executable Python framework for multi-hop retrieval reasoning: the reasoning trace is a program over retrieval and QA tools, runtime exceptions drive self-repair, and retrieval expands when the evidence is insufficient.

PyRAG-RL: 39.3 average exact match across four QA benchmarks, +6.9 over reported Search-R1

8How Much Can Language Models Gain from Test-Time Computation?

How much can test-time computation improve a language model, and at what cost? Test-time scaling is widely proposed as a substitute for larger models, but existing comparisons mostly evaluate one domain at a time and rarely charge selection to the budget. We introduce SELF-POT, a benchmark and evaluation framework that measures the test-time potential of a model across competition mathematics, competitive programming, and agentic workflows. SELF-POT separates candidate coverage from final accuracy on static tasks, tracks correctness transitions under revision, and measures protocol completion alongside task success in agentic environments. Under a unified budget rule, it compares Direct inference with parallel sampling and self-revision under fixed multiples of the Direct budget, and charges every model call, including selection and critique, in dollars. This design supports two kinds of comparison: the gain a model obtains from additional inference, and a lower-cost model with additional inference against a stronger model. Across five low-cost reasoning models on 350 sealed tasks, with Claude Opus 5.5 Direct as the reference, the returns depend on the domain, the selection rule, and failure handling. When we replay the retained programming candidate pools, public-example selection raises correct submissions from 376 to 453 of 500 scheduled cells while saving 12-49% of logical API cost across models, and simply retaining an available candidate when judging fails recovers 61 submissions at unchanged cost. On identical mathematics pools, judging with fallback yields 186 correct submissions versus 182 for voting, while voting saves 12-21% of logical API cost. These controlled replays show how selection and failure handling change the gains realized from the same generated candidates, and they quantify the marginal value of a model judge.

Bangji Yang, Jingyuan Li, Jiajun Fan, Yi Evie Zhang, Ruihan Guo, Hongbo Ma, Neil He, Chumeng Liang, Qinglong Zheng, Zhanghan Ni, Ge Liu

A budget-controlled evaluation of test-time computation across 350 mathematics, programming, workflow and terminal tasks. Controlled pool replays separate candidate coverage from submission accuracy.

Public-example selection raises correct programming submissions from 376 to 453 of 500

9Keystone: Judging Previews at Key Steps for Test-Time Search in Diffusion Models

Text-to-image diffusion and flow models yield a preview, their clean-image estimate, at every denoising step. When they miscount, drop or misplace objects, test-time search is in a bind: its verifiers are reward models built for finished images, so judging early misreads blurry previews, and judging late, as best-of-N does, pays to fully denoise doomed candidates. We find that the image is not the problem: the outcome is settled as denoising starts, by the seed or by a stochastic sampler’s first steps, and already shows in early previews; what fails on them is the judge, as reward models read them near chance on several kinds of prompts. We introduce Keystone, a training-free judge of previews that compiles a prompt into typed questions about its objects, counts, colours and positions and has an off-the-shelf multimodal decision model answer them. Asked this way, the judge reads counts and positions from early previews better than reward models read them from finished images. Searching at these key steps on GenEval with SD3.5-Medium, Keystone’s adaptive search and pruning significantly outperform best-of-8 with every other verifier at under half its generator compute, and as a drop-in verifier there, the Keystone score significantly improves best-of-N, progressive seed pruning and FK steering; without retuning, both searches stay significantly above the strongest other verifier’s best-of-N on average over four base models and three benchmarks. Keystone thus moves test-time judgment to the key steps where the outcome already shows.

Jiajun Fan, Chaoran Cheng, Ziwen Wang, Changyi Yang, Bangji Yang, Qinglong Zheng, Ge Liu

A training-free verifier that compiles a prompt into typed questions and has an off-the-shelf multimodal decision model judge intermediate clean-image predictions, combined with adaptive search and pruning.

Exceeds best-of-8 with other verifiers at less than half the generator compute (GenEval, SD3.5-Medium)

10What Drives the Prediction? How Neural Models Rely on Physical Information

When energy can be decoded from a hidden layer, does the model use that information to predict the next state? Matched Invariant Interchange (MII) tests whether exchanging units correlated with energy makes the predicted energy track a donor’s energy more closely than exchanging equally many control units. We call this difference in correlation selectivity. Our contributions are: i) Calibrated physical transfer. A small selection gap has two interpretations. In residual predictors, even a whole-layer exchange transfers little donor energy. In direct predictors, the whole layer can transfer energy although its most energy-correlated units transfer less than random units. ii) Representation-dependent selectivity. Across three sizes of the Poseidon fluid model, channels selected for kinetic energy outperform random channels in the encoder and penultimate decoder. At the final decoder, whole-layer transfer is nearly complete, yet selected channels transfer less than random ones. iii) Training-dependent physical selectivity. On NS-Sines, supervising Poseidon-B on kinetic energy rather than a fixed random function of the field raises encoder selectivity by 0.066–0.134 across three seeds. Subtracting the control response changes two of three checkpoint choices and lowers mean kinetic-energy error on both test distributions. On NS-Gauss, selecting by validation field error instead yields a more accurate velocity field.

Chih-Ting Liao, Zhikai Pan, Qinglong Zheng, Huqin Weng, Chunlei Meng, Xin Cao

Matched Invariant Interchange tests how energy-correlated hidden units affect the physical quantities in a model's predictions, using matched donor and control-unit interventions.

On NS-Sines, energy supervision raises encoder selectivity by 0.066–0.134 over random-function supervision across three seeds

11Keel: A Frontier Model Teaches a Vision-Language Model by Rewriting Its Weights

Can a frontier model repair a vision-language model (VLM) by rewriting its weights? Small reasoning VLMs such as Qwen3.5 need such a repair: in thinking mode they fall into loops on many image questions, repeating themselves until the token limit, spending the whole budget and losing the answer. The usual remedies act on the decoding, leaving the looping model as it was, or must be told what to change. Yet the cause is written in the weights. Keel, the first agentic weight-editing method for VLMs on image tasks, finds and removes it there. Told only the symptom, an editor, a frontier multimodal LLM acting as an agent, locates the cause, edits the weights and tests them, with a failure census and instruments showing it the failing images and where the VLM attends; among several editors, the harness keeps only edits that clearly reduce the failure without lowering accuracy on questions no editor saw. The repair needs no training, keeps the model’s size and per-token cost, and applies to already-edited weights. The editors expose where the loops live: from the original weights, independent editors on both models trace them to the attention outputs of the middle layers, and the smaller VLM stops consulting the image inside a loop. On Qwen3.5-0.8B and Qwen3.5-2B, across six image benchmarks from visual mathematics to GUI grounding, the edit removes most loops on every benchmark and shortens answers, and the edited models are significantly more accurate than the thinking models on average; the larger one gains most on GUI grounding, a task absent from the practice questions. Because the repair lives in the weights, it combines with budget forcing where held-out questions favor it: on the best available weights, Keel has the best average accuracy on both models, significantly above every training-free baseline we compare on the larger one.

Jiajun Fan, Neil He, Bangji Yang, Qinglong Zheng, Ziwen Wang, Chaoran Cheng, Changyi Yang, Hanqi Mao, Ge Liu

A frontier multimodal agent diagnoses thinking loops in a small vision-language model, edits its weights, and validates the repair on held-out questions, all without gradient training.

Qwen3.5-0.8B and 2B on six visual reasoning and GUI grounding benchmarks: fewer loops, higher average accuracy, same size and per-token cost

12HINGE: From Cross-Object Spillover to Selective State Editing

Vision–language models can recognize object states, such as whether a box is open or closed. Editing their interpretation of one object’s state raises a harder question: will counts and other dependent answers update consistently while facts about unedited objects remain intact? We introduce HINGE, a benchmark that tests these requirements on rendered scenes and natural photographs. Its questions distinguish the requested state change, its consequences, and facts that should be preserved. Intervention experiments characterize cross-object state spillover, where editing one object changes a previously correct state answer about another. We further introduce Swing Editor, which keeps the model frozen and learns one additive vector per desired state, applied to the selected object’s visual features. Evaluation spans seven models: Qwen3.5 (4B–27B), Gemma 4 (E2B and 31B), and InternVL3.5 (4B–38B). Swing Editor uses approximately 0.27–0.33% of the trainable parameters of low-rank adaptation (LoRA) on Qwen3.5-4B and 9B. Trained only on single-object edits in synthetic scenes, it correctly answers every scene question after two simultaneous edits in 82.5–97.5% of trials, averaging 91.8% versus LoRA’s 81.3% across the seven models. Without photograph training, it achieves 56.2–79.0% success on real photographs, requiring both the requested state and all available preservation checks to be correct. Adding photograph training raises this to 74.8–88.1%. With additional double-object supervision, mean scene success reaches 96.4%. Comparisons with object-local LoReFT and RED expose complementary composition and preservation trade-offs.

Chih-Ting Liao, Yitong Qiao, Qinglong Zheng, Huqin Weng, Fei Shen, Xin Cao, Tat-Seng Chua

A benchmark that tests requested object-state changes, their consequences, and the preservation of unaffected facts in synthetic scenes and natural photographs, plus Swing Editor, which learns object-local state vectors with the VLM frozen.

91.8% mean double-edit scene success across seven VLMs after single-object training, vs 81.3% for LoRA

13Self-Upgrading Embodied Agents

An embodied agent built on a language model goes to work with the weights it was given and keeps them for life. To get better, it needs more training, or reflection, memory, search or prompts that cost every step and are lost when the task changes. Editing weights by reasoning, weight-space programming, could end this, but has stayed with models that only reason or relied on a far larger commercial model. We introduce the self-upgrading embodied agent, to our knowledge the first that learns from its own experience by upgrading its own weights on the job, between its tasks, with no training, no gradient, no reward model, no second model, no test-time scaling and no harness or prompt engineering. By day a 27B agent works in a household environment. At night it reads its record, finds many of its steps wasted on replies that reach the token limit before any action, traces the habit to one direction in its hidden states, and rewrites one tensor of its weights. An agent that rewrites itself must first never break itself, so it is its own examiner: it tests every change against its unchanged self on practice tasks and keeps only what holds. When an edit holds, the next day it is a better agent, at the same cost per step: over thousands of held-out episodes, 48 fresh seeds a task, its one kept edit makes it complete significantly more tasks, in fewer steps, with fewer wasted and fewer tokens. This self-improvement is multi-round, stable and continual: through the nights that follow, the upgrade holds, without collapse. Of four sizes of its family, only the 27B agent does this. From here on, an embodied agent need not keep training and inference apart: as it works, it gathers its own experience and upgrades itself, with no separate pre-training or post-training and no human intervention. We believe this opens lifelong continual learning for robots: stable, continual, recursive self-improvement.

Jiajun Fan, Neil He, Qinglong Zheng, Changyi Yang, Tong Wei, Andy Mao, Zetian Li, Chaoran Cheng, Hui Ren, Ruihan Guo, Xianrui Zhong, Xizheng Yu, Jingyuan Li, Xi Zhu, Kaiqu Liang, Chang Xu, Zun Qian, Zidi Xiong, Yuezhu Zhao, Xu Huang, Ge Liu

An embodied agent that learns from its own experience by rewriting its own weights between tasks, with no gradient, reward model or second model. It traces wasted steps to one direction in its hidden states, edits one attention layer, and keeps the edit only if it holds against its unchanged self on practice tasks.
